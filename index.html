"Different Kinds of Venting":
College students turn to generative AI in lieu of traditional therapy and friends

Young people are coming to artificial intelligence models for emotional support as professionals are concerned about overuse of artificial intelligence and potential risks.

By: Alessandra Shires, Charlie Bloomer, Emily Wang, Tiffany Yuan

Barnard College

Dating in New York City can be stressful. For some, the fast paced nature of the city can call for additional emotional support and validation. Cecilia, an international master's student from China at Columbia, talked to a familiar voice. But instead of sitting on a cushioned couch and relaying her emotions to a living therapist, Cecilia poured her qualms into the generative artificial intelligence model ChatGPT.

College students are venting issues into generative AI models such as Claude and ChatGPT to alleviate stress, and in some cases, use AI as a replacement or as an alternative to therapy. Young adults faced with intense challenges, transitions and important life decisions see the automated models as an antidote to anxiety, discomfort with friends or other mental health struggles.

"In New York I'm not familiar with the dating culture and I got hurt several times," Cecilia said. "I'm also getting used to it by using ChatGPT." She adds it's not ChatGPT alone that helps, but moreover the ability to vent to someone who is not emotional. "I need a person to be there to hear all my stuff. That's it."

This nationwide trend is reflected in data from researchers at RAND, Brown University School of Public Health and Harvard, who argued that "nearly one in eight young people" are using AI chatbots, such as ChatGPT for mental health advice. The data, which focused on a study of 18 to 21 year olds, pointed out that over 90% of students said that the report was helpful.

This was corroborated by the students we spoke to. University of Toronto student, Nawal Shebe, a first-year and prospective Geography and Environmental Management major, frequently uses AI for advice and comfort. "It's kind of scary how easy it is to get answers so quickly and how reliable it feels," Shebe said.

Faced with intense academic pressure, she's asked ChatGPT about her future if she were to drop out. "How would I even go about that, so I don't end up being like, unsuccessful or like homeless or something [in] 10 years," she said. "I want to ask that [things] are on a larger scale."

Professionals raise concern

Although the technology has received positive responses from young people, professors and psychologists remain concerned about the use of the technology.

"I've taken a pretty strong stance against it," Nathan Gorelick, an assistant professor at Barnard College and author of the book The Unwritten Enlightenment: Literature between Ideology and the Unconscious shared his thoughts with us on why some may be skeptical about the use of AI. He argues that it takes away from the learning process in his classes when he finds AI generated essays.

But when asked about AI being used for student's wellbeing Gorelick paused thoughtfully. "I wouldn't have moral judgment on students," Gorelick began. "I am wary of the capitalization of wellness. I think the human mind is the social mind. I do think there are dangers to the automation or organization of mental health."

Kimberly Satin Kubler, a clinical social worker with a LCSW-C licence explained to us that while AI therapy might be useful in simple approaches (such as one-off conversations) it can have damaging long-term consequences depending on the type of therapy.

"When people say, AI therapy is just like it to me, it sounds like just a completely different thing than my understanding of therapy," she said. She told us that understanding the physiological — body language and ability to relate — processes of a person is an important part of psychological training.

"AI doesn't have eyes," she said. "I feel concerned about psychosis. If somebody has serious trauma or complex trauma. It isn't able to help that person get out of that state, which that's concerning to me."

It is important to note ongoing critiques from a broader scale. "It also comes amid reports that OpenAI is facing seven lawsuits alleging that ChatGPT drove users to delusions and suicide," the report from the Brown School of Public Health stated.

One of the potential reasons is the validating nature that AI is designed to have. "I do think AI is kind of designed to give you answers that you're looking for," Kubler said.

But for some students, that type of reiterating validation is all they're looking for. "Sometimes I feel like I just need to make my thoughts clear," Cecilia said. "I already have the answer in my heart, in my mind, I just need to make it more clear and talking with ChatGPT can help me with that. And then I just shut up and I just turn it off."

The appeal of an always-on listener

The busy schedules of college students can further exasperate the desire for an immediate fix. "I talk to it a lot is because you can talk to your friend, right? But your friend is not always available. They have other plans, schedules, it can not always be there for you, but ChatGPT can," Cecilia said. She added that she appreciates that AI models are not emotional the way a friend can be.

Rena (name changed for privacy), a sophomore at Barnard, shared similar experiences using AI Chatbot rather than consulting a therapist. "I thought about booking off-campus therapy sessions in Midtown, but I never managed to go." The 25-minute commute during midterm season, combined with the possibility of long wait times at the therapist's office, discouraged her from attending sessions and led her to turn to AI Chatbots as a more efficient alternative.

For young people, doubts do appear in the use of AI, such as data phishing. "I mean, there's a lot of times where I'm like, Oh, maybe I shouldn't put this information about myself, or maybe I shouldn't be so specific about this because, you know, I don't know the capabilities of it," Shebe said.

Kubler warned us in our interview against students establishing a relationship with AI. "People are starting to really believe that they have a relationship with AI, and that's obviously not true, and that seems damaging."

Learning to rely on a machine

Like Shebe, Cecilia originally used the AI models as an academic tool. Cecilia relies on three AI models heavily in order to conduct macro research for her master's program: Claude, ChatGPT and DeepSeek. But she turns to ChatGPT the most for emotional support.

"The reason I use ChatGPT is because I've been using it for two years and I'm familiar with his voice," Cecilia said. "He will always say, 'You're doing it well, you're doing it right and so don't do that. [Don't] punish yourself.'"

Cecilia shared that she returns to ChatGPT over other models because of the familiarity with "his" voice. She assigned a gender to the AI model. This anthropomorphic act highlights the assigned characteristics to a language model that can't relate back.

Rena shared how she found out AI Chatbots possess humanistic characteristics. As both ChatGPT and Claude user, she identified strengths of both chatbots: ChatGPT has good memory and sends you emojis, similar to how people text in real life; Claude's responses are especially reassuring when it comes to difficult situations.

Shebe's background in using AI to vent was originally academic. "In the beginning, I actually tried my best not to [use AI]," she said. She started relying on AI around 10th grade, first asking it questions for school assignments and academic work. Over time she became more dependent on it: as a senior in high school, she started turning to the model in moments of social isolation.

"It's like different kinds of venting," Shebe said. The first year found that in moments of career anxiety where she's searching for pragmatic advice about the future, she turns to AI. But in moments of emotional distress, she turns to her friends for help. Shebe also added the social isolation of her respective university pushed her to use AI for mental health.

But this perfect friend with such a newly under-researched background remains skeptical to professionals. "AI might be really good at giving," Kubler said. "A kind of a perfect answer to somebody. It takes humanity out. A therapist would have an emotional reaction to a patient. If you are really well trained, that's a part of therapy."

The messy, human aspects of therapy — the ability to relate to another person and understand how to pull someone out of a traumatized state — are two shortcomings of artificial intelligence. Despite this, young people faced with mental health issues and complex transitions continue to use AI for their immediate comfort.

Methodology

The survey used for this article was distributed anonymously online and received 23 responses from college students. Questions included both multiple-choice and short-response formats covering AI usage patterns, emotional reliance, perceived effectiveness, and barriers to human connection. Participants were recruited through direct outreach to college students at multiple institutions. The sample is not representative of the broader college population; findings should be read as exploratory rather than statistically definitive. All named interview subjects agreed to speak on the record. Names of survey participants have been replaced with pseudonyms to protect their privacy.
