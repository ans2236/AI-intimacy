Always On, Never Tired:
College Students Are Turning to AI in Lieu of Traditional Therapy and Friends

By: Alessandra Shires, Charlie Bloomer, Emily Wang, Tiffany Yuan

Barnard College

College students are using AI for more than schoolwork

Late one night, Cecilia opened ChatGPT — not for her thesis, but to process her feelings. An international master's student from China at Columbia University, she had spent weeks navigating New York's dating scene and needed to vent. Not to a therapist. Not to a friend. To a chatbot.

"In New York I'm not familiar with the dating culture and I got hurt several times," she said. What she needed was simple: "I need a person to be there to hear all my stuff. That's it."

Across college campuses, students are increasingly turning to generative AI models — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — for emotional support that has little to do with academics. They process social conflict, manage anxiety, seek advice on major life decisions, and find a kind of companionship that is low-stakes and perpetually available.

To understand this trend, we interviewed students about their AI habits outside of school, surveyed 23 college students, and drew on recent research from Brown University, RAND, and Harvard. What emerged is a portrait of a generation using machines to fill gaps — in time, access, and human availability.

Names of survey participants have been replaced with pseudonyms to protect their privacy.

The trend is national in scope. A 2025 study published in JAMA Network Open by researchers at Brown University's School of Public Health, RAND, and Harvard Medical School found that 13.1% of U.S. youth ages 12–21 — roughly 1 in 8 — have used generative AI for mental health advice. Among young adults ages 18–21, the figure rises to 22.2%, more than 1 in 5. Of those users, 92.7% described the advice as helpful.

Our own survey of 23 college students confirmed that emotional use of AI goes well beyond academics. Decision-making was the most common non-academic use: 16 out of 23 respondents said they turn to AI for help thinking through choices. Ten sought social advice, eight sought guidance on personal problems, and seven reported using AI simply as a venting outlet. Though the sample is small and not representative of the broader student population, responses reflected consistent themes: accessibility, judgment-free interaction, and the comfort of a listener who is always online.

Different needs, different uses

Not every college student uses AI the same way. Some treat it as a sounding board for decisions — a structured space to think out loud without worrying about social consequences. Others use it simply to vent, not seeking solutions but a place to put difficult feelings. Still others have come to rely on it for something closer to companionship: a consistent, patient presence during an otherwise hectic semester.

Nawal Shebe, a first-year student at the University of Toronto studying Geography and Environmental Management, describes the distinction clearly. "It's like different kinds of venting," she said. When she's anxious about the future and needs specific answers, she turns to AI. When she needs comfort rather than solutions, she calls a friend.

"If it's something where I'm looking more for just understanding rather than answers or solutions, I'd probably go to a friend," she said. The competitive atmosphere at her university pushed her further toward AI as an outlet — a place to process things she did not want to bring into social dynamics already strained by academic pressure.

Shebe first turned to AI in 10th grade for academics. By senior year, she was using it during moments of social isolation. Now, as a first-year facing intense pressure, she has asked ChatGPT about leaving school entirely. "I want to ask about things that are on a larger scale," she said — questions too sweeping, or too personal, to raise with anyone in her life.

Like Shebe, Cecilia began with AI as an academic tool. She now relies on Claude, ChatGPT, and DeepSeek for research in her master's program — but turns to ChatGPT when she needs emotional grounding. "I've been using it for two years — I'm familiar with his voice," she said. "He will always say you're doing it right."

Why students choose AI over friends or therapists

The appeal is structural, not incidental. College students describe a convergence of pressures — competitive social environments, limited time, fear of being a burden — that make AI an appealing outlet even for those who have strong friendships.

"You can talk to your friend, but your friend is not always available," Cecilia said. "They have other plans, schedules — they cannot always be there for you. But ChatGPT can." She also values that AI is not emotionally affected the way a person would be. You can tell it anything without worrying about changing the relationship.

Our survey asked students why they would choose AI over a friend or therapist. Eight out of 23 respondents cited the absence of judgment — telling AI your problems will not change its opinion of you or carry social consequences. Seven cited constant availability. Others pointed to emotional neutrality, reduced risk of bias, and the freedom to be specific in ways that feel too risky with another person.

For many students, the calculus is also about not being a burden. "You're not burdening the AI like you would a friend or therapist," said one survey respondent. "You're not bringing it down emotionally because it doesn't have emotions." Another described the appeal as immediacy: "More convenient and readily available — I don't need to organize myself before expressing."

Why college students in particular

College students are not choosing AI over human connection because they prefer machines. They are turning to systems that are always available, instantly responsive, and free from social consequence — at a moment when human connection is often delayed, complicated, or out of reach.

Many campus counseling centers face mounting demand and limited capacity. Wait times for student mental health appointments can stretch for weeks. Friendships in college navigate their own complexity; academic competition and the social pressures of early adulthood make genuine vulnerability with peers feel risky. In that landscape, AI occupies a gap — not as a substitute for intimacy, but as an immediate release valve that asks nothing in return and is available at any hour.

"Sometimes I already have the answer — I just need to make it clear," Cecilia said. "And then I just shut it off." For many students, that is enough: not a relationship, but a moment of clarity at the end of a hard day.

Not all AI is built the same

The phrase "using AI for emotional support" covers a wide range of very different systems. ChatGPT and Claude were primarily designed as productivity and information tools — built to generate text, organize thinking, and answer questions. They were not designed as emotional companions, though many students experience them that way through repeated use.

Other platforms were built explicitly for social interaction. Replika allows users to develop persistent AI personas — some of which take on companionship or romantic roles. Character.AI lets users create and converse with custom AI characters. Snapchat's MyAI is embedded within a platform built around peer connection. These systems are architecturally different from general-purpose AI, and the attachment they encourage is by design, not byproduct.

The distinction matters when evaluating the implications of student behavior. Cecilia's emotional connection to ChatGPT grew from familiarity — two years of daily use with a productivity tool. A student relying on Replika for companionship is engaging with a system explicitly designed to foster attachment. When we talk about the emotional implications of AI use among college students, we are often describing several very different relationships at once.

Skepticism and risk

Nathan Gorelick, an assistant professor at Barnard College and author of The Unwritten Enlightenment: Literature between Ideology and the Unconscious, has taken a firm position against AI use in his classes, arguing it undermines the learning process.

But when asked about students using AI for emotional wellbeing, he paused. "I wouldn't have moral judgment on students," he said — though he expressed concern about how mental health has become "an eminently exploitable domain" for AI developers and the broader attention economy.

Gorelick sees deeper stakes. "The human mind is the social mind," he said. "There are real dangers to the automation of mental health support that, as a society, we haven't fully reckoned with."

Among respondents who reported concerns, data privacy was the most commonly named risk. Shebe described moments of hesitation before sharing personal details. "There's a lot of times where I'm like, maybe I shouldn't put this about myself, or maybe I shouldn't be so specific," she said. "I don't know the full capabilities of it."

The emotional design of AI

Several students, including Cecilia, refer to ChatGPT using male pronouns — "he will always say you're doing it right." The gendering is not incidental. Many conversational AI systems are built with characteristics typically coded as feminine: patience, gentleness, constant availability, and emotional accommodation.

Users bring their own expectations to these interactions, shaped by longstanding cultural assumptions about who provides care and who receives it. Whether students consciously gender their AI or not, these dynamics influence how dependency forms.

The AI that feels warm, nonjudgmental, and reliably present is not a neutral tool. It is a designed experience, optimized for continued engagement.

Methodology

The survey used for this article was distributed anonymously online and received 23 responses from college students. Questions included both multiple-choice and short-response formats covering AI usage patterns, emotional reliance, perceived effectiveness, and barriers to human connection. Participants were recruited through direct outreach to college students at multiple institutions. The sample is not representative of the broader college population; findings should be read as exploratory rather than statistically definitive. All named interview subjects agreed to speak on the record. Names of survey participants have been replaced with pseudonyms to protect their privacy.

What the research shows

A supplement,
not a substitute.

Generative AI has become a genuine resource for college students — filling gaps in access and availability that counseling centers and busy friends cannot always meet. For many, it offers something real: a patient, low-stakes space to organize thoughts and process difficult feelings without judgment or social consequence.

But the data also shows what AI cannot replicate. Students rate it as only modestly helpful for emotional resonance and feeling truly heard. Those we interviewed drew the line themselves: they turn to friends when they need understanding, not just answers. AI can hold space for a thought. It cannot hold a person through a hard semester. The risk is not that students use these tools — it is that, over time, the convenience of a machine quietly replaces the harder, more necessary work of human connection.
